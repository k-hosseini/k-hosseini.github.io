I am a PhD candidate in Economics at [Simon Fraser University](https://www.sfu.ca/economics.html), expected to complete my degree in 2027. I am an applied microeconomist working on **immigration, labour, and political economy**.

My research uses large administrative and survey datasets together with quasi-experimental methods to study questions that matter for policy. My job market paper uses linked Canadian immigration and tax records to compare the long-run earnings of immigrants who first came to Canada as international students with those of immigrants admitted directly as permanent residents. Other projects study how the roll-out of 3G mobile internet changed women's employment in Iran, and how Western economic sanctions changed voting and dissent in Iran.

Before my PhD, I worked as a data analyst on applied policy studies in Tehran. During my PhD, I interned in the Social Statistics and Economics program at Statistics Canada. I have also worked with confidential Statistics Canada microdata as a deemed employee through the Canadian Research Data Centre Network (CRDCN). I have taught Data Visualization as a sessional lecturer at SFU and have worked as a teaching assistant for courses from first-year principles to econometrics.

**Fields:** Labour Economics, Immigration, Political Economy, Applied Econometrics

Job Market Paper
======

**[Immigrants' Pathways to Success: Study Permit vs Permanent Residency](/research/#job-market-paper)** (with Fernando Aragón)<br>
[Slides](/files/Hosseini_Aragon_Immigrants_Pathways_Slides.pdf)

Which immigrants a country admits, and how it helps them integrate, are central questions in immigration policy. Using the Longitudinal Immigration Database linked with annual tax files, we compare the long-run economic trajectories of immigrants who arrived on study permits and later became permanent residents with those of immigrants who arrived directly as permanent residents. Former students earn less than direct permanent residents during their first eight years after admission, but their incomes overtake those of direct permanent residents in later years.
